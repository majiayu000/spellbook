# Directing Product Proof

Use this reference after product inspection and before writing a timed script.

## 1. Choose the decision spine

Write one sentence that can reject attractive but irrelevant footage:

`This demo turns [audience doubt] into [confidence] by showing [observable product transformation].`

Cascade it through the production:

| Layer | Decision test |
|---|---|
| Feature selection | Does this capability materially answer the doubt? |
| Action | Does the product visibly change state or produce evidence? |
| Framing | Can the audience locate the cause and result without searching? |
| Narration | Does it add value, consequence, or context the screen cannot? |
| Sound | Does it redirect attention or register a result? |
| Cut | Does the next image add, compare, compress, or resolve information? |

## 2. Calibrate against a reference

Choose one concrete artifact before designing. Compare it on:

- native product surface versus explanatory graphics;
- first meaningful product action;
- observable events per ten seconds;
- longest unchanged frame and longest audio void;
- shot-scale, crop, and color progression;
- real operator behavior, friction, response, and consequence;
- sound continuity and landing energy.

Extract two or three qualities to preserve. A genre label such as “Apple-like,”
“cinematic,” or “high energy” is not calibration.

## 3. Design the change

Define:

```text
Opening product state:
Opening audience belief:
Obstacle or doubt:
Turning proof:
Landing product state:
Landing audience belief:
Next action or call to action:
```

If the opening and landing states are equivalent, the demo is structurally
flat. A collection of unrelated finished screenshots does not create change.

## 4. Select proof, not coverage

Use three depths:

1. **Core proof**: show the complete cause, product action, and consequence.
2. **Differentiator**: show why the product is meaningfully different.
3. **Supporting evidence**: establish trust quickly without breaking the flow.

An exhaustive feature list may still be delivered as a coverage appendix. It
should not dictate equal screen time.

For each feature ask:

- What doubt does it remove?
- What exact product state changes?
- What can the camera or terminal actually capture?
- What evidence proves it is not a staged animation?
- What is the shortest truthful action chain?

## 5. Build chapters and beats

Use chapters when the user goal, proof question, or visual condition changes.
Use beats when at least one changes:

- the operator adopts a new tactic;
- the product accepts a meaningful input;
- the audience receives decisive information;
- the product produces or persists a result;
- an error, gate, or recovery path redirects attention;
- the immediate question changes.

Record the audience state before and after every beat. Remove a beat when those
states are identical and the product state does not change.

## 6. Hook with native action

For a promotional demo, prefer a legible result in the opening seconds, then
return to the credible starting state. The hook should create a question the
body can answer, not spend the best result before the audience understands it.

Useful hook shapes:

- before/after transformation;
- failure or friction followed by the finished result;
- one-command input followed by a surprising real output;
- rapid result montage from one coherent user journey;
- a high-stakes question immediately answered by product evidence.

Avoid company history, logo animation, setup narration, or permission dialogs
as the first meaningful image unless one of them is the product's value.

For a promotional software demo, require a meaningful native product action by
five seconds. A claim card about a result does not satisfy this requirement.

## 7. Control attention

Give every shot one primary job. Use the smallest device that makes it legible:

- stable wide frame for orientation;
- crop or zoom for a decisive detail;
- cursor path only when cause depends on pointer location;
- highlight or callout for a result that would otherwise be missed;
- chapter card when the proof question changes;
- split screen only for a genuine comparison;
- contact sheet or result montage only after the relationship is established.

Do not stack zoom, cursor halo, caption, callout, and motion background around a
simple result. Visual hierarchy should become clearer as proof accumulates.

Count observable events inside the shot: input, response, reveal, result,
motivated crop, sound cue, or cut. During the hook, aim for a new event every
0.8–2 seconds; during the body, every 1.5–3 seconds. Do not count ambient drift,
blinking cursors, progress spinners, or decorative particles.

## 8. Align narration and action

Assign each channel a job:

- **screen**: how the product behaves;
- **narration**: why the action matters or what changed;
- **on-screen text**: names, metrics, commands, or conclusions that must remain
  inspectable;
- **UI/action sound**: cause, completion, or state transition;
- **music**: larger pressure and release, never missing product logic.

Start the corresponding action before or shortly after narration creates the
expectation. Land on the result as the sentence resolves. If processing takes
time, show a readable state change, compress it transparently, or redesign the
proof path.

## 9. Shape pace

Choose `max_information_gap_seconds` for the audience and format rather than
copying a universal number. Useful starting points:

| Format | Starting range |
|---|---:|
| 15–60 second promo | 3–6 seconds |
| 60–180 second product demo | 5–8 seconds |
| Longer technical walkthrough | 8–15 seconds |

This limit governs meaningful information, not arbitrary cuts. A moving cursor
or animated spinner is not new information. A held result is valid when the
audience needs time to read, compare, judge, or absorb it; record that reason in
the beat plan.

## 10. Reject template signals

Redesign when several of these appear together:

- equal-duration beats unrelated to action length;
- repeated centered headlines and symmetrical cards;
- abstract benefit copy where behavior should be visible;
- a single dark-gradient palette with decorative neon accents;
- no cursor, typing, state transition, failure, latency, or consequence;
- product output retyped into a fake terminal or presentation layer;
- identical shot scale and composition across changing claims.

## 11. Review the sequence

For each planned cut complete:

`Cut from [state/action/sound] to [new state/action/sound] because the audience now needs to [learn/feel/locate/compare/anticipate].`

Reject cuts that only decorate, and reject long takes whose internal action
does not develop the proof.
